About

Most founders I notice aren't struggling because their product is bad or their market is wrong. They're struggling because the way they're running the company hasn't kept up with where the company is.

Every decision goes through them. The team waits. Work piles up. Growth starts feeling like chaos instead of progress.

That's not a people problem. That's a systems problem.

I get inside the business, find where the friction is actually coming from, and fix it. Not a deck. Not a recommendation. The actual fix. I work based on outcomes, not time. I move as fast as the work allows without compromising on quality, and I'm not leaving until what we agreed to get done is done.

No retainers. No open ended roles. Every engagement is scoped to a specific outcome with a price tied to that scope. You know exactly what you're getting and what it costs before anything starts.

This is not freelance work. Every engagement runs on a documented methodology, a written scope agreed before anything starts, and a defined completion condition that tells both sides exactly when the job is done. The system is built to be rigorous every time, not just when it feels right.

How an engagement works
  • 01
    The founder interview We talk. Structured, focused, 30 to 45 minutes. I'm not selling and you're not pitching. I'm listening for the gap between what you think the problem is and what it actually is. By the end I'll tell you whether I think I can help and what working together would look like.
  • 02
    I get inside the business If we decide to move forward, I spend one day to one week inside the company. This phase requires a commitment from both sides before it begins. I look at how decisions get made, where work actually stalls, what the team dynamic reveals under the surface. At the end I deliver a written Scope Report: what I found, what it means, what needs to change, what it costs, and what done looks like.
  • 03
    Written scope. Agreed price. Before anything starts. I deliver a written document defining exactly what changes will be made, why, what it costs, and what done looks like. You approve it or you don't. If you don't, we part ways professionally. If you do, that document is what I'm held to.
  • 04
    I execute Hands-on work. No handoffs. You always know where things stand: I report progress, risks, and decisions made on a weekly cadence. If new constraints surface during execution I surface them to you immediately. You decide whether to address them. If yes, a new scope is agreed. If no, I stay within what was originally approved and deliver against it.
  • 05
    Clean exit Once the outcome is achieved I confirm it in writing: what was agreed, what changed, and what the company looks like now. Then I leave. No retainer. No ongoing role. If a new constraint surfaces later, we start fresh.
Who I work with

I work with whoever holds the highest operational authority in the company: the founder, CEO, or in board-led companies, the person the board has designated to make decisions. The engagement requires that level of buy-in. It cannot be driven from below it.

The right fit is a company that has clarity on what it sells and has moved past the earliest stage. Product is defined, there's some traction, and the focus has shifted from building to scaling. That's where operational friction tends to show up most.

If the business is still finding its footing on what to build or who to sell to, the timing probably isn't right. But once that's clear and execution starts feeling harder than it should: that's the conversation worth having.

There is no standing retainer and no ongoing embedded role. But the business remains a trusted operator that clients come back to when new constraints arise, when momentum slows, or when a high-stakes execution moment needs the right person in the room.

This isn't dependency. It's the opposite. The goal is to leave the company running better than before I arrived, so the next time we work together it's by choice, not necessity.

The moments that typically bring clients back: the company crosses a headcount threshold and decision-making breaks down again. A new product line launches and the execution system wasn't built for it. A first leadership hire (a COO or VP) joins and needs a clean authority handoff. Revenue plateaus after a growth phase and nobody can name why. These aren't emergencies. They're the natural friction points of a company that's moving.
